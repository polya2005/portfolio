# FPGA Spinthesizer

_This page contains excerpts from the final report for the project._

## Introduction

The _FPGA Spinthesizer_ is a real-time musical system that converts MIDI input into audible tones generated by stepper motors.
The system is implemented entirely on an FPGA fabric and integrates a modular MIDI processing pipeline with a scalable array of stepper-motor output generators.
Incoming MIDI packets are decoded, routed, and optionally transformed through a pseudo-random improvisation engine that enables call-and-response musical interaction.
These processed notes are mapped to motor-driving waveforms using a precomputed note-to-period conversion module and a dedicated hardware signal generator, allowing stepper motors to produce musically accurate pitches.
Our implementation harnesses the RealDigital Urbana Board, an FPGA-based prototyping platform that uses the AMD Spartan-7 XC7S50-CSGA324 FPGA.

## System Description

![High Level Block Diagram](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/block-high-level.png)

At the high level, the `midi_processor` module of our Spinthesizer takes in MIDI data, optionally adds its own improvisation to the data, and parses everything into the frequencies and volumes of the notes to be synthesized. The parsed data is then used by the `stepper_out_gen` module to generate control signals for the stepper drivers. The enable signals are serialized and passed to an external 24-bit shift register as the PMOD header does not contain enough pins to directly output all the required signals.

My contribution includes the Stepper Output Generator, the Serializer, and the drive electronics.

### RTL Design

#### Playing One Note at Various Volumes

![Note Driver Block Diagram](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/block-note-drv.png)

To play a note with a given MIDI note number $n$, the note number is converted to the period of the note, in number of clock cycles, using the equation

$$
N_n = (12\:231\:220.59)2^{-\frac{n}{12}}.
$$

This is implemented in the `nn_to_period` module as a lookup table taking 3072 bits of distributed RAM (&lt;0.5% of available resource).

Next, the `stepper_drv_sig_gen` module produces a suitable signal for the external A4988 stepper drivers to step the motors every $N_n$ clock cycles. Additionally, the module also instructs the A4988 drivers to reverse the direction every time the note changes in order to achieve a visual effect.

Lastly, different volumes can be implemented by varying the number of stepper motors playing the same note. This design supports 4 volumes (0, 1, 2, and 3) plus one "not playing" volume.

#### Playing Multiple Notes at Once

![Stepper Output Generator Block Diagram](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/block-sog.png)

In an ideal world, playing multiple notes at the same time is as simple as instantiating multiple `note_driver`s and routing the output signals to the driver electronics.

However, we do not have enough PMOD pins to route all the signals to the drivers. Hence, we serialize the `en` signals and feed the serialized data to three cascaded SN74HCS5 shift registers to output 24 required enable signals. We only use shift registers for the `en` signals because they are not timing sensitive, and this is enough to overcome the pin number constraint.

### Electronics Design

![Top-level Schematic Diagram](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/sch-top.png)
At the highest level, the circuit consists of a 12V power input, a reset button, PMOD headers to carry the signal from the FPGA board, three shift registers (as mentioned above), many A4988's to drive the stepper motors, and a UART input to interface with the MIDI instrument. The UART data is simply connected to one of the PMOD pins.

The three shift registers are chained to make a 24-bit shift register for 24 enable signals. Four enable signals along with the `DIR` and `STEP` signals from the FPGA are passed to each of the 6 available note drivers.

Please note that bypass capacitors are omitted from this diagram to avoid clutter.

![Low-level Schematic Diagram](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/sch-ll.png)
Each note consists of four motor and driver pairs. Each pair receives identical `DIR` and `STEP` signals from the FPGA. In contrast, the `EN` signals could be different for each driver, depending on the volume of the note being played.

Each motor and driver pair is configured according to typical application of the driver module. The MS1, MS2, and MS3 pins are all pulled down to disable microstepping. Again, bypass capacitors are omitted in this diagram to avoid clutter.

### PCB Design

I designed the PCB for this project in KiCAD.
![PCB](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/pcb.png)
Important considerations for the PCB design include:

- The A4988 stepper drivers require a 12V power supply, which is provided by a 12V wall adapter. The PCB includes a barrel jack (and a power switch) for this purpose. The traces for the 12V power supply are made wider to handle the current drawn by the stepper motors.
- The clock signal for the shift registers is provided by the FPGA board. The clock signal is routed to all three shift registers, and the traces are kept as short as possible to minimize signal degradation. The three clock signal traces are also length-matched to ensure that the clock signal arrives at all three shift registers at the same time.
- To facilitate debugging, the PCB includes test points for the driver signals, the clock and data signals for the shift registers, the MIDI signals, and the power rails. The test points are placed in a convenient location on the PCB, and are labeled for easy identification.

### Mechanical Design

![Mechanical Design](/projects/fpga-spinthesizer/mech-design.png)
Using Fusion 360, I designed a 3D-printed stand and a laser-cut acrylic mounting plate for the stepper motors in addition to colorful 3D-printed flags to visualize the motor positions.
